# Anti-AI tells

The goal is not "avoid these words." It is to understand why each pattern
signals machine-written text, so you can recognise the instinct when it shows
up in a form not listed here.

The underlying reason most AI captions read as fake: they are **written about
the experience from outside it**, by something summarising and drawing a
conclusion. A person who actually ate the dinner does not summarise the dinner.
They mention the one thing they noticed. Everything below is a symptom of that
one root cause.

## Structures to avoid

**The abstract-noun subject.** "Balance is not about restriction." "Consistency
beats intensity." The sentence has no person in it. She was there; the caption
should show it. Name the food, the weight, the time, the actual thing.

**Not X, but Y.** "It's not about the calories, it's about how you feel." This
construction almost never appears in unselfconscious writing. It is the shape
of an argument, and she is not arguing.

**The rule of three.** "Simple, filling, and full of protein." Three balanced
items in a row is the single most recognisable tell. Real people list two
things, or four uneven ones, or one.

**The closing lesson.** A caption that ends by explaining what the post meant.
Her real captions end on the last item of the list. Stopping without a moral is
the most human thing a caption can do.

**The hook question.** "Ever wonder how I stay on program while eating African
food?" Nobody talks like this. If there is genuine tension in the post, state
it flat: "third plate of jollof this week. still on program."

**Colon-then-payoff.** "The truth about meal prep: it's easier than you think."
Reads as content marketing, because it is.

**Over-explaining the photo.** The image already shows the plate. A caption
that narrates it is filling space.

**Engagement bait.** "Drop a 🙌 if you agree." "Who else?" "Tell me in the
comments." It is transparently a request for engagement, and her real captions
never ask for anything.

## Words and phrases that give it away

Not banned outright - if she says one naturally, it is hers. But each one
should have to earn its place, because these are the words that cluster in
machine-written wellness copy:

`journey` · `embrace` · `nourish` · `fuel your body` · `elevate` · `unlock` ·
`game changer` · `glow` · `thrive` · `intentional` · `mindful` · `radiant` ·
`effortless` · `curated` · `obsessed` · `can't get enough` · `let's dive in` ·
`level up` · `hits different` · `the girlies` · `it's giving` · `main character`

The trend words are their own problem: `hits different`, `it's giving`, `era`
and their successors date a post to the month it was written, and read as
someone reaching for a voice rather than having one.

## Punctuation and formatting

**Em dashes.** Fine in prose, but in a short social caption a dramatic pause
mid-sentence is a writerly move, not a texting move. Use a full stop or a line
break.

**Perfect grammar in a casual register.** Correctly punctuated captions with
no fragments read as drafted. Her real sample has "Salmon sauce" in it, which
is not a dish name and is exactly right. Do not tidy her up.

**Emoji on every line.** One emoji, placed once, is her. A decorated caption is
not.

**Symmetry.** Sentences of matching length and rhythm stacked in a row. Real
writing is lumpy - a long line, then three words.

**Title Case Everywhere.** Capitalising Every Word Of A Sentence reads as a
graphic, not a caption. Her header line takes light title case. Nothing else
does.

## What reads as genuinely human

The positive version of all of the above:

- **Specifics with numbers and names.** "Two sweet potatoes" beats "a serving
  of complex carbs." Specificity is the fastest signal that someone was there.
- **Admitted contradiction.** "I said one plate. It was three." Machines
  resolve tension; people just report it.
- **Mid-thought openings.** Starting as though continuing a conversation.
- **Fragments.** "Third time this week. No notes."
- **Unresolved endings.** Stop when the information runs out.
- **Small, useless, true detail.** The thing that serves no purpose except
  that it happened. This is the hardest to fake and the most convincing.

## The test before handing it over

Read the caption and ask: **could this have been written by someone who was not
there?** If yes, it is not finished. Put in the detail only she would know, and
take out the sentence that could sit under anyone's photo.
